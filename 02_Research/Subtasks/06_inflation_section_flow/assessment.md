## 1. Bottom line

The plan has the right spine: inflation as the reason to act, placed right before the decision. Four things would make it much stronger.

1. **Frame the section as an update to the Oct 5 call.** The Oct 5 deck recommended a hold and named its own trigger: *"Hike if core inflation reaccelerates or expectations drift."* Core momentum has since reaccelerated. If the team now recommends a hike, the inflation section's job is to show that the trigger fired. That is exactly the "previous call → dated change → evidence → revised setting" pattern the notes credit to Pace. It's the most persuasive structure available to you, and the plan doesn't use it yet.
2. **Add the two beats the plan is missing: expectations/second-round effects, and a forecast with revision triggers.** These are what separate teams under the rubric (forecast and risk analysis). Without them the section describes inflation but doesn't forecast it.
3. **Don't make breadth carry the argument alone.** Breadth is a good slide, but it's a threshold measure. Our own refresh shows one heavy item (OER) can swing it, and the trimmed-mean reading points the other way. Pair it with persistence measures and confront the contrary reading on the same slide. Also, the breadth numbers sit on a pipeline with an open core-classification error.
4. **Tighten the AI beat and give it one job.** Two channels over two slides, placed between breadth and the decision, risks the problem the notes flag for Penn: material whose link to the rate decision isn't clear. Fold it into one slide that answers "does the AI build-out make inflation more or less likely to fade on its own?", and consider whether the demand half belongs in the growth section.

The recap (point 2) needs a change in kind, not just length. Don't set the vulnerabilities aside; condense them into one-line risk judgments that the decision reuses. The hold and 50 bp cases live in those vulnerabilities.

## 3. The plan, point by point

### Point 1. Order: inflation last, right before the decision — **Keep, with conditions**

*Judgment:* this is right for a hike recommendation. Inflation is the "why act"; growth and labor are the "can we afford to act." Putting the why next to the decision makes the decision feel like a conclusion rather than a pivot.

Evidence from finalists is mixed and doesn't settle it:
- **Pace (1st):** output → labor → inflation → financial conditions → stance → decision. Inflation was late-middle, followed by financial conditions. That's the closest precedent to your order.
- **Harvard (2nd):** inflation second, right after activity.
- **UCLA (3rd):** opened with the tariff shock and its inflation implications.

Ordering didn't decide placement. What the top three share is that each lens ended in a judgment the decision reused.

Conditions:
1. **The decision question must appear in the opener.** Otherwise the inflation-last order buries the stakes (see §2, first row).
2. **Decide where financial conditions go.** The Oct 5 deck's own hold case leaned on financial tightening: "5-yr real yield +80 bp since June, after the Sept hike." Your STATE hunches add bond yields and the recent equity decline. That's the strongest argument that policy is already doing work. If financial conditions sit before inflation, the recap must carry it. If they're dropped, the hold case loses its best premise.
3. **This is a CORE decision.** It changes the other speakers' sections, especially whether labor and growth end on judgments your section can pick up.

*Strongest counterargument to inflation-last:* if the judges lose the thread in the first two sections, the most important material comes when attention is lowest, and Q&A time is the same either way. The fix is the opener, not reversing the order.

### Point 2. Recap before inflation — **Keep the device, change its content**

*The concern.* Dropping the vulnerabilities (AI capex concentration, labor-force changes, immigration) removes the material the alternatives depend on:
- The **hold** case is "the September hike plus tighter financial conditions are working, and labor is softer than the headline."
- The **50 bp** case is "growth is strong enough to absorb it."
Both live in the vulnerabilities. If the recap says only "growth strong, labor at max," the hold case has nothing to stand on when the decision arrives, and the structured disagreement turns into a strawman.

*Recommended form: a risk ledger, one line per lens.* For example (wording illustrative, each lens owner writes theirs):
- Growth: strong, carried by AI investment → risk: concentration.
- Labor: near maximum employment, no slack building → risk: hiring has cooled; workers expect higher unemployment.
- Financial conditions: tighter since September → risk: equity-driven pullback in spending.

Then: "Against that backdrop, the question is inflation." The decision slide reuses the same lines. This is the Pace pattern.

*"At maximum employment" is a claim judges will test.* Project data support "near" rather than "at":
- *Fact* (Codex 2025 notes, 2026 snapshot section): Oct payrolls +165k, U-3 4.1%; the Oct 28 FOMC statement described strong activity and stable unemployment.
- *Fact* (STATE.md, from AI-research subtask 03, Oct 20): prime-age employment-to-population ratio 80.7%, flat over the year.
- *Contrary fact:* the Oct 5 deck said job finding "has weakened materially" (Chicago Fed hiring rate for unemployed). The NY Fed survey in the Codex notes shows the expected probability of higher unemployment rising.

"Near maximum employment, with no slack building" is defensible. "At maximum employment" invites "how do you know?" That should be a labor-lens decision, but your section inherits the claim.

### Point 3. Headline and core PCE at 12/6/3 — **Keep; fix the transition and the data frontier**

*Your assumption: inflation has picked up since Oct 5.* **Supported by project data.**
- Oct 5 deck (Aug data): headline 12m 3.4%, 3m annualized 1.0%; core 12m 3.0%, 3m 2.0%, 6m 2.7%. The slide title was "Still Elevated, but Momentum Has Cooled."
- Now (Sep PCE, released Oct 29): core 12m 3.1%, 6m 2.9%, 3m 2.9%; headline 12m 3.65%, 3m 3.6%.
- Oct CPI (Nov 10): core 3m annualized about 3.75%, with core at 0.3% m/m for three straight months.
- Caveat: part of the Aug → Sep move in core 12m is revision (Aug 3.01 → 3.03), not new inflation.

So the momentum story has reversed. That's the trigger the Oct 5 deck named, and you should say it plainly.

*Your assumption: gasoline and energy were the inciting factor.* **Supported for headline; not established for core.**
- Headline: gasoline went from about −31% to +47% 3m annualized (Aug → Sep), then +72% in Oct CPI. Gasoline and energy goods are the largest single contributor to 12m headline PCE.
- Core: the largest 12m core PCE contributors are health care, housing, financial services, transportation services and recreational goods. The Oct CPI core 3m acceleration is led by OER, wireless, lodging, rent and airfares. Airfares plausibly link to fuel. OER, rent and wireless don't.
- *Judgment:* the honest transition is "energy explains the headline jump; the question for policy is whether it is leaking into core." That makes the FEDS note a hypothesis to test in the next slide, not a conclusion.

*The FEDS pass-through note* (you asked me to remind you when oil comes up). Per STATE.md:
- It's a 5-economy panel. The main text has no US-only estimates. It's reduced form: it can't separate oil supply shocks from oil demand shocks, and oil may be proxying broader demand.
- The authors call their projection an upper bound.
- **The second half (country results, policy simulation, footnotes) is still unverified because the fetch was truncated.** Read it before citing anything beyond the headline result.
- Davidson's pitfall applies. If you cite it, also name the US observable that would confirm pass-through, e.g. contributions from energy-intensive core services, or PPI pipeline pressure reaching core PCE.

*The data frontier needs a decision.* October PCE is released **Nov 25, after the Nov 18 cutoff**. Sep is the last official PCE.
- "Latest core PCE" on Nov 20 will be two months old, while October CPI and PPI are in hand.
- Options: show Sep PCE plus an October nowcast with provenance (Cleveland's, or our own build in `04_oct_pce_build`), or show Sep PCE and Oct CPI side by side.
- Showing a labeled nowcast is Pace-style transparent forecasting and preempts "what about October?" It only works if the provenance is airtight and audited.

*Slide count.* 12/6/3 for both headline and core is six numbers on one chart. The Oct 5 slide already used that design, which also makes the before/after comparison easy.

### Point 4. Breadth — **Keep, but don't let it carry the argument alone**

*Judgment:* breadth is a good lens and a continuation of the Oct 5 theme ("Holding Our Breadth"; breadth slide "Broad and Plateaued Since March"). But as the core of the argument it has three weaknesses a judge can exploit.

**(a) It's a threshold measure, and our own data show it's fragile.**
- Core CPI 3m breadth jumped from 17% to 58% of weight between Aug and Sep. Mostly that's OER (about a third of the weight) crossing the 3% line (2.80 → 3.22).
- Oct breadth direction depends on the weighting choice (flat on exact 3m weights, up on 12m-base weights).
- Core PCE 12m breadth is about 60%, flat since August; 3m breadth rose from 34% to 45%.
- *Judgment:* "breadth rose" can be true and still be driven by one item. Show weighted contributions next to the share, so the room can see whether the pressure is broad or heavy.

**(b) Breadth isn't persistence.** Your plan asks breadth to answer persistence too. These are different questions:
- Breadth: how many prices are rising fast.
- Persistence: whether they'll keep rising.

Measures closer to persistence that the project already has:
- Atlanta sticky core CPI (12m about 3.1%; 3m annualized about 3.8%).
- Supercore PCE (services ex energy and housing: 12m 3.6%, 3m 3.4%).
- Market-based core PCE.
- Harvard's use of market-based services ex housing is the precedent here.

**(c) The strongest contrary reading isn't in the plan.**
- Dallas trimmed-mean PCE is about 2.3% (12m, Sep) against core PCE at 3.1%: a gap of about 0.9 pp. Its trim count also changed sharply (upper trim 70 → 85 components).
- Cleveland median CPI is about 2.8%.
- *Worth a look:* about 60% of core PCE weight is above 3% on 12-month component rates, while the 12m trimmed mean is about 2.3%. The two are built differently (12-month component changes vs. monthly trimming, compounded), so this isn't a contradiction on its face. But it's the first question a sharp judge asks: "Your breadth says broad; the trimmed mean says the typical price is near 2.3%. Which is it?" Research needs to answer that before the slide is built. (Logged to `04_Ideas/questions_log.md`.)

**(d) Measurement: the breadth numbers aren't slide-ready yet.**
- The price-basket pipeline has an **open core-classification error**: 16 components mislabeled, pipeline core weight 90.9% vs. BEA's 88.9%. The fix is briefed in `05_fix_core_flag`, which STATE.md says you'd run in another window.
- Any core breadth number should be rebuilt after that fix.

*Spillover question.* "What are the spillover effects of the oil shock?" is the right question. Breadth alone can't answer it, because breadth doesn't know why a price rose. The upstream data in the project do bear on it:
- PPI final demand +1.3% m/m in Sep (largest since Mar 2022) and +6.3% y/y.
- Unprocessed intermediate PPI +21.5% y/y.
- Oct PPI arrives Nov 13, before the cutoff.

*Judgment:* a pipeline view (crude → intermediate → final demand → consumer) is a cleaner spillover test than breadth, and it's a distinctive slide.

*Something that cuts the other way:* financial services is the third-largest contributor to 12m core PCE. Much of that is portfolio-management fees, which track equity prices. If equities have fallen (your STATE hunch), that component mechanically drags on coming core PCE prints. It's a forecast point and a Q&A trap: part of "core" momentum is a stock-market artifact, not demand pressure.

### Point 5. AI build-out, two channels — **Keep the topic, restructure, probably one slide**

*What's right.* Judges raised productivity in the semifinal (STATE.md), so you have to address it. The two-channel idea (disinflation from productivity, inflation from the build-out) is the correct tension.

*What to change.*

1. **Order the channels by time, not by sign.** The finalists' advice (outline item 3) is to separate what investment does now (demand), what realized productivity and unit costs can establish, and what future evidence would change potential-growth or neutral-rate assumptions. For the decision, timing is the point. Build-out demand is here now. Productivity disinflation is partly here, uncertain and not attributable to AI. A central bank sets policy for the next 12–18 months.
   - Demand now (*fact*, demand refresh): hyperscaler cash capex up about 108% y/y in Q3; data-center construction up about 80%; real information-processing equipment +29% y/y.
   - Supply evidence now (*fact*): Q3 productivity +3.6% q/q annualized and +2.1% y/y; unit labor costs −0.7% q/q annualized and +2.4% y/y.
   - Attribution (*fact*, STATE.md subtask 12): BLS has no separate AI measure, and BEA's AI statistics are experimental. Realized productivity can't be attributed to AI.
2. **Add the neutral-rate link. It's the bridge to the decision that the plan doesn't have.** An investment boom raises the neutral rate. If neutral is higher, the current rate is less restrictive than it looks, which strengthens the hike case. This is also where the finalists got questions: three of six 2025 teams were asked about the neutral rate in Q&A. *Assumption:* the team has someone ready to handle r-star. Your Sept 21 notes flagged a possibly higher r-star as speculation.
3. **Unit labor costs are the inflation-relevant productivity number.** ULC growth about 2.4% y/y and ECI wages about 3.1% y/y say labor costs aren't the inflation engine right now. That's an important piece of evidence for the hold case, and it belongs in the second-round section (§4.1) as much as in AI.
4. **Narrow the cost channel to what reaches consumer prices.** Chip prices mostly hit investment goods, not the PCE basket. Electricity is the consumer-facing channel (CPI electricity +3.8% y/y in Aug, per the Oct 5 deck). Stating that limit yourself preempts the "how much of that is in PCE?" question.

*Placement.* Two options:
- **(A, my lean)** One AI slide at the end of the inflation section, framed as "Will inflation fade on its own?" Demand now says no. Supply later says maybe. Neutral rate says policy may be looser than it looks. Then into the decision.
- **(B)** Put build-out demand in the growth section (it's growth evidence) and keep only the unit-cost and neutral-rate implications in yours. Less duplication, a shorter inflation section, but AI then can't land as a single argument. This depends on what the labor and growth owners are planning for productivity. The Oct 5 deck had the productivity/ULC slide in the employment section.

Ask before choosing: this is a coordination question, not an economics question.

### Point 6. Policy decision — **Keep, and add triggers and the hold case's real premise**

- **Give each option its strongest premise in the deck** (Pace; outline item 4).
  - *Hold:* energy is a supply shock policy should look through; underlying measures (trimmed mean) are lower; wages and unit costs aren't accelerating; market expectations are anchored; the September hike, higher real yields and lower equities are already tightening.
  - *50 bp:* momentum has reversed across horizons; upstream PPI is very hot; household expectations are well above the norm; post-pandemic pass-through may be larger; inflation has been above target for years, so credibility costs rise.
  - *25 bp:* the trigger fired, but evidence on second-round effects is mixed, so act and keep optionality.
- **Name triggers in both directions** (Harvard; Pace's 30-second trigger). What would make you pause after 25? What would make you go 50 or keep hiking? Each should be an observable with a date: e.g. Oct PCE on Nov 25, Michigan long-run expectations, core services momentum.
- **Say which meeting you're advising.** *Assumption:* December 2026. Harvard said its meeting explicitly. Last year, teams that didn't left ambiguity.
- **Use the real FOMC split.** Per the Codex 2026 snapshot, the Oct 28 FOMC held at 3.75–4.00% with two dissents favoring a hike. I haven't checked that against the statement myself. If confirmed, your structured disagreement mirrors the Committee's actual one, which is worth one sentence.
- **Balance sheet and communication.** The Oct 5 recommendation had a balance-sheet line (keep buying bills for ample reserves) and a communication line. Pace changed its QT call in Q&A when the evidence changed. Decide whether yours changes and say so; judges asked three of six 2025 teams about the balance sheet.

## 4. What's missing, in priority order

### 4.1 Expectations and second-round effects — **add (high priority)**

This is the biggest gap. The plan goes from momentum to breadth to AI without asking whether inflation is becoming self-sustaining through expectations or wages. That's the question that justifies a hike over a look-through. Pace's reversal trigger was longer-run expectations drifting up.

Project data already show a split that's tailor-made for the competing-evidence technique (*facts*, Nov 10 refresh):
- **Households are elevated:**
  - Michigan 1-year 4.3% and 5–10-year 3.4% (Nov preliminary; Oct final 4.7% / 3.5%).
  - NY Fed SCE 1/3/5-year 3.9% / 3.3% / 3.0% (Oct).
- **Markets are anchored:** 5-year breakeven 2.25%, 5y5y forward 2.15% (Nov 10).
- **Labor costs aren't accelerating:** ECI total compensation 3.4% y/y, wages 3.1% (Q3); unit labor costs 2.4% y/y.

*Judgment:* one slide, "Is it spreading through expectations and wages?", with households on one side and markets plus wages on the other. Your call on which side wins is the hinge of hike vs. hold. Without it, the hold side can say "it's an energy shock and second-round effects are absent," and the presentation has no answer.

Also: the Q4 SPF is due mid-November, before the cutoff. The Oct 5 deck used SPF and the Cleveland model.

### 4.2 An inflation forecast with provenance and scenarios — **add (high priority)**

The rubric note in CLAUDE.md says forecast and risk analysis separate teams, and the required chain ends in *forecast → policy consequence → revision trigger*. The plan has evidence and interpretation, but no forecast.

Minimum version:
- A baseline path for core PCE through 2027 from labeled sources (September SEP, Q4 SPF, the Oct nowcast).
- An upside scenario (oil pass-through persists; the FEDS note's projection is explicitly an upper bound).
- A downside scenario (oil reverses; equity-linked services drag).

Pace's lesson: label how the paths were built and be ready to explain units and interpolation in 20 seconds. The Oct 5 deck put forecasts in a separate section 5. Decide whether the inflation forecast lives there or in your section. *Judgment:* yours, because it's the bridge to the decision.

### 4.3 Upstream pipeline pressure — **add, possibly merged into breadth/spillover**

PPI final demand and intermediate PPI are the cleanest project evidence on whether the energy shock is moving through the supply chain (§3, point 4). Oct PPI lands Nov 13.

### 4.4 Measurement and vintage backup slide — **add (backup, not spoken)**

This year's data are messier than usual:
- Oct CPI 12m is an approximation on an interpolated Oct 2025 base.
- Oct PCE comes after the cutoff.
- BEA changed methods for several components on Sep 30.
- Cleveland and Atlanta publish Oct 12m figures BLS doesn't.

One slide with release dates, sources and transformations is the finalists' outline item 5. Keep one dated release sheet for all speakers (the Harvard payroll-count slip in the Q&A notes).

### 4.5 Distribution tie-in — **one sentence, not a slide**

Your hunch and the Oct 5 distribution section say lower-income households are hit hardest through gasoline and food. One line in the decision ("persistent inflation is regressive") reuses the distribution lens without duplicating it. The Oct 5 recommendation already had it in the communication line.

### 4.6 What the plan can cut or compress

- **The pass-through transition as a standalone slide.** It works better as one spoken sentence plus a citation, given the unverified second half and the panel-not-US caveat.
- **Six-series momentum charts.** If time is tight, keep core at 12/6/3 and headline at 12m only.
- **AI as two slides.** One slide (§3, point 5).

## 5. A revised flow to react to
